How it works

01 · The idea

A satellite cannot trust a cost sheet written on a laptop.

A satellite that runs AI onboard must decide, frame by frame, how much of a small and shifting energy, memory and time budget each inference deserves.

Adaptive inference systems usually choose from a cost menu profiled once, on a development machine. On our Raspberry Pi 4B payload that menu was about 6.5× too optimistic, so a controller that trusted it planned for deadlines it could not meet.

MIZAN (ميزان, Arabic for balance) weighs accuracy against the energy and time it costs, using costs measured on the board it runs on, and keeps correcting them while it flies. Its safety filter can refuse a configuration but never chooses one.

Three choices per frame

Model depth
Exit early from a MobileNetV3-Small network when it is confident; go deeper when it is not.
Frame frequency
Run or skip a frame, weighed by its value: clear land is worth more than cloud, ocean or night.
Input quality
Process the image at 48 or 64 pixels.

How MIZAN decides, every frame

Each step is shown with one real decision from the mission twin: decision 187, over Germany, with the Tunis link open. Simulated

  1. 1

    Observe state

    Battery, temperature, deadline, memory and the value of the frame.

    deadline 40.0 ms · clear land, value 1.0 · 26.8 °C · 4,066 J usable

  2. 2

    Propose configuration

    The most valuable option the energy price allows, from the corrected menu.

    early-exit 64 px: 6.2 ms × 6.29 = 39.2 ms > 38.0 ms budget → exit 1 · 64 px

  3. 3

    Safety veto

    Refuse anything whose bound breaks the deadline, battery or heat limit. It can veto; it never chooses.

    believed 11.9 ms, fits the budget · no veto

  4. 4

    Execute

    Run the chosen exit and resolution on the board, or skip the frame.

    exit 1 at 64 px on the payload computer

  5. 5

    Measure actual cost

    Time what the board really took.

    measured 26.8 ms · deadline 40.0 ms · on time

  6. 6

    Update the cost menu

    Fold measured over predicted into a conformal latency bound for the next frame.

    measured ÷ menu → correction updated

Why the energy lever is weak on the payload: from a published Pi 4 base draw (1,518 mW), a declared 2.0 W active increment, the 5 s frame and the slowest mean latency measured (26.95 ms), one inference is about 0.7 % of a frame's energy, and 0.2–3.4 % across any plausible mismatch. On a duty-cycled payload, how often to run matters more than how deep.

05 · Paper

MIZAN: Measure-First Adaptive Inference for Energy-Constrained Satellite Payloads

Ahmed Hedhili, Salem Essid, Ahmed Mnasri · IASTAM 6.0 Technical Challenge · 25 pages with appendices, IEEE format.

We test the three levers an adaptive inference system has on a satellite: which model to run, how often to run it, and how much detail to process.

MIZAN pairs a calibrated early-exit network at two resolutions with a controller that prices energy in closed form from a profiled cost menu, corrected online, behind a safety filter that can veto but never choose.

Three findings follow. The model lever saves 52–57 % of energy per inference only on saturated accelerators. A laptop-profiled menu is 6.5× optimistic on the Raspberry Pi 4B, the computing board of the BUPT-1 CubeSat. Correcting it online cuts missed deadlines by 49 % on the board, while the cheapest fixed option still misses fewer, which sets the next step.
